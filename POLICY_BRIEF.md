# Policy Brief: Chronic Health Conditions and Voter Turnout — How Diabetes Creates Barriers to Civic Participation

## Executive Summary

Chronic health conditions such as diabetes mellitus impose significant burdens on quality of life, daily functioning, and social participation—factors that extend well beyond the clinical setting and into the civic sphere. Evidence from clinical trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov demonstrates that diabetes diminishes physical mobility, emotional well-being, social engagement, and functional independence. These burdens translate into reduced capacity to participate in civic life, including voting. This policy brief synthesizes clinical trial evidence to argue that policymakers must recognize chronic disease management as a determinant of democratic participation and take concrete steps to reduce voting barriers for individuals living with diabetes and other chronic conditions.

---

## 1. Background

Diabetes affects over 38 million people in the United States and an estimated 537 million adults worldwide. Beyond its well-documented clinical complications—cardiovascular disease, kidney failure, neuropathy, and vision loss—diabetes profoundly impacts patients' daily lives. Managing diabetes requires ongoing self-care behaviors (diet, exercise, medication adherence, blood glucose monitoring), frequent medical appointments, and navigation of complex healthcare systems. These demands, combined with the physical and emotional toll of the disease, can constrain individuals' ability to engage in activities beyond self-care—including community participation and civic duties such as voting.

Voter turnout among individuals with chronic health conditions is consistently lower than among healthy populations. Research suggests this disparity is driven by physical limitations, transportation barriers, time constraints from medical appointments, psychological burdens (depression, anxiety, diabetes distress), and reduced social participation. Understanding these barriers is essential for designing inclusive electoral policies.

## 2. Clinical Trial Evidence

The following clinical trials, sourced from ClinicalTrials.gov, provide evidence that diabetes and related chronic conditions impair quality of life, daily functioning, and social participation—the very capacities necessary for civic engagement, including voting.

### 2.1 The MODEL Program (NCT02957513) — Quality of Life and Social Burden in Underserved Diabetes Patients

- **Title:** The Management of Diabetes in Everyday Life Program (MODEL)
- **Sponsor:** University of Tennessee; funded by the Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Institute (PCORI)
- **Population:** 646 African-American adults with uncontrolled diabetes and multiple chronic conditions living in medically underserved communities
- **Design:** Pragmatic randomized controlled trial comparing text messaging, health coaching, and enhanced usual care
- **Key Findings and Relevance:**
  - The study explicitly measured **diabetes-specific quality of life** using the Diabetes-39 instrument, which includes domains for **social burden**, anxiety and worry, sexual functioning, and energy and mobility.
  - The study population—individuals with uncontrolled diabetes, multiple comorbidities, and living in underserved areas—represents a group at high risk for reduced civic participation due to compounded health and socioeconomic disadvantages.
  - Secondary outcomes included **primary care engagement** (delayed needed care, usual source of care), reflecting the time and logistical burdens of chronic disease management that compete with civic activities.
  - **Citation:** ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT02957513. Published: Bailey JE et al., *J Gen Intern Med.* 2026;41(6):1573-1583.

### 2.2 REAL Diabetes Study (NCT02214641) — Social Functioning and Activity Participation in Young Adults

- **Title:** Resilient, Empowered, Active Living with Diabetes (REAL)
- **Sponsor:** University of Southern California; funded by NIDDK/NIH
- **Population:** 81 young adults (ages 18-30) with type 1 or type 2 diabetes and poor glycemic control (A1C ≥ 8.0%)
- **Design:** Randomized controlled trial of an occupation-based lifestyle intervention
- **Key Findings and Relevance:**
  - The study measured **quality of life using the Audit of Diabetes-Dependent Quality of Life (ADD-QoL)**, a 19-item survey assessing the impact of diabetes on **social, physical, and emotional functioning**.
  - Secondary outcomes included **diabetes-related distress** (PAID Scale), **depression** (PHQ-8), **life satisfaction** (SWLS), and **activity participation**—all of which are directly relevant to the capacity for civic engagement.
  - The published results demonstrated that the intervention improved both glycemic control and quality of life, indicating that diabetes management directly affects social and emotional functioning.
  - **Citation:** ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT02214641. Published: Pyatak EA et al., *Diabetes Care.* 2018;41(4):696-704.

### 2.3 Interprofessional Community-Based Diabetes Intervention for Older Adults (NCT01880476)

- **Title:** Feasibility, Acceptability and Effects of an Interprofessional Community-Based Health Promotion Intervention on Self-Management in Older Adults With Type 2 Diabetes and Comorbid Chronic Conditions
- **Sponsor:** McMaster University
- **Population:** 45 older adults (age 65+) with type 2 diabetes and at least two other chronic conditions, living independently in the community
- **Design:** Single-group pilot study with a 6-month interprofessional home visit and group program intervention
- **Key Findings and Relevance:**
  - Measured **health-related quality of life** (SF-12v2), **depressive symptoms** (CES-D), and **anxiety** (GAD-7)—all of which influence the physical and psychological capacity to participate in voting.
  - The intervention included **care coordination** to help participants access necessary supports and services, highlighting the logistical complexity of managing multiple chronic conditions that can crowd out civic activities.
  - The focus on community-dwelling older adults with multiple chronic conditions underscores how compounded health burdens in aging populations reduce the capacity for independent community participation.
  - **Citation:** ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT01880476. Published: Markle-Reid M et al., *Pilot Feasibility Stud.* 2016;2:24.

### 2.4 Michigan Men's Diabetes Project III (NCT06574035) — Social Support and Quality of Life in Black Men with Diabetes

- **Title:** Michigan Men's Diabetes Project III (MenDIII): Mind and Motion
- **Sponsor:** University of Michigan
- **Population:** 80 Black men with type 2 diabetes, A1C ≥ 7.0%, with depressive symptoms
- **Design:** Randomized controlled trial of CBT plus exercise versus CBT alone
- **Key Findings and Relevance:**
  - Primary outcomes include **general quality of life** (SF-12) and **diabetes-specific quality of life** (Diabetes Quality of Life Instrument), measuring satisfaction, worry, and the **impact of diabetes on daily life**.
  - Other outcomes include **diabetes-related distress**, **depression** (PHQ-9), and **social support** (Duke/UNC Functional Social Support Scale)—factors directly tied to the ability to engage in community and civic life.
  - The study addresses a population (Black men with diabetes and depression) that faces intersecting barriers to both health management and civic participation, including systemic health disparities and social marginalization.
  - **Citation:** ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT06574035. Status: Recruiting.

### 2.5 PROVEN-DIA (NCT06426277) — Quality of Life and Social Factors in Diabetes Prevention

- **Title:** Effectiveness of the Brazilian Diabetes Prevention Program (PROVEN-DIA)
- **Sponsor:** Beneficência Portuguesa de São Paulo
- **Population:** 1,305 adults at high risk of type 2 diabetes (prediabetes) across Brazil
- **Design:** Multicenter randomized controlled trial of lifestyle intervention (hybrid and telehealth) versus usual care
- **Key Findings and Relevance:**
  - Explicitly measures **quality of life across eight SF-36 domains**, including **social functioning**, **role limitations due to emotional problems**, and **role limitations due to physical health**.
  - The study analyzes **social, cultural, educational, and geographical factors** at the community level and their association with diabetes incidence, recognizing that social determinants shape both health outcomes and civic capacity.
  - By including quality of life and social functioning as primary secondary outcomes, this trial shows that even prediabetes—a precursor to diabetes—is associated with diminished social participation.
  - **Citation:** ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT06426277. Status: Recruiting.

### 2.6 Peer-Delivered Integrated Illness Management and Recovery (NCT04481737) — Disability and Social Support in Chronic Disease

- **Title:** Peer-delivered and Technology-Assisted Integrated Illness Management and Recovery
- **Sponsor:** Dartmouth-Hitchcock Medical Center
- **Population:** 40 adults with serious mental illness and chronic health conditions including diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and obesity
- **Design:** Randomized controlled trial comparing peer-delivered technology-assisted intervention to routine peer support
- **Key Findings and Relevance:**
  - Measured **functional ability** (PROMIS Global Health), **disability** (London Handicap Scale, including domains of mobility, physical independence, occupation, social integration, orientation, and economic self-sufficiency), and **social support** (Medical Outcomes Study Social Support Survey).
  - The London Handicap Scale's domains of **social integration** and **economic self-sufficiency** directly parallel the capacities needed for voting—physical independence to access polling places, social integration to remain connected to civic life, and economic self-sufficiency to overcome financial barriers to participation.
  - **Citation:** ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT04481737. Published: Fortuna KL et al., *Mhealth.* 2025;11:28.

### 2.7 Internet-Delivered CBT for Chronic Conditions (NCT03500237) — Disability and Quality of Life

- **Title:** Effectiveness of Internet-delivered Cognitive Behaviour Therapy for Chronic Conditions
- **Sponsor:** University of Regina
- **Population:** 188 adults with chronic health conditions (including diabetes, heart disease, cancer) and symptoms of anxiety or depression
- **Design:** Randomized controlled trial comparing team-guided vs. self-guided internet-delivered CBT
- **Key Findings and Relevance:**
  - Measured **disability** (WHO Disability Assessment Schedule-12), **quality of life** (EQ-5D-5L), **life satisfaction** (SWLS), and **self-efficacy** for managing chronic disease.
  - The WHO-DAS captures domains of functioning directly relevant to civic participation, including understanding and communicating, mobility, self-care, interpersonal interactions, life activities, and participation in society.
  - This trial demonstrates that chronic conditions like diabetes create measurable disability that extends into all areas of life, including social and civic participation.
  - **Citation:** ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT03500237. Published: Mehta SH et al., *J Behav Med.* 2022;45(5):674-689.

### 2.8 Weight Management for Adults with Mobility-Related Disabilities (NCT04046471) — Barriers and Social Support

- **Title:** Weight Management for Adults With Mobility Related Disabilities
- **Sponsor:** University of Kansas Medical Center; funded by NIDDK/NIH
- **Population:** 129 overweight/obese adults with permanent mobility-related disabilities (including conditions related to diabetes complications such as stroke, lower limb amputation)
- **Design:** Randomized controlled trial comparing individual home-visit vs. group remote weight loss intervention
- **Key Findings and Relevance:**
  - Measured **quality of life** (SF-36), **barriers to exercise** (Barriers to Exercise for Disabled Persons scale), and **social support** (Social Support and Exercise/Diet Survey).
  - The trial explicitly addresses **transportation barriers** by delivering interventions in-home or remotely—mirroring the transportation barriers that prevent many individuals with chronic conditions from reaching polling places.
  - The population includes individuals with mobility disabilities stemming from chronic disease complications, representing a group facing compounded barriers to physical civic participation.
  - **Citation:** ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT04046471. Published results available on ClinicalTrials.gov.

## 3. How Diabetes Creates Barriers to Voter Turnout

Based on the clinical trial evidence above, diabetes creates barriers to voter turnout through the following mechanisms:

### 3.1 Physical Limitations and Mobility Restrictions
Diabetes complications—neuropathy, foot ulcers, amputations, vision loss, and cardiovascular disease—directly impair mobility. Trials like NCT04046471 demonstrate that adults with mobility-related disabilities face significant barriers to physical activity and community participation. The same barriers that prevent walking outdoors or attending group sessions also prevent traveling to polling places, standing in lines, and physically accessing voting booths.

### 3.2 Time and Logistical Burdens of Disease Management
The MODEL trial (NCT02957513) and the McMaster interprofessional intervention (NCT01880476) highlight the extensive time demands of diabetes self-management—frequent medical appointments, medication management, care coordination, and self-monitoring. These time-intensive activities compete directly with the time needed for civic activities such as researching candidates, requesting absentee ballots, and traveling to vote.

### 3.3 Psychological Burdens: Depression, Anxiety, and Diabetes Distress
Multiple trials (NCT02214641, NCT06574035, NCT03500237) document high rates of depression, anxiety, and diabetes-specific distress among individuals with diabetes. These psychological burdens reduce motivation, energy, and the sense of self-efficacy needed to overcome the administrative and logistical hurdles of voting, particularly in states with restrictive voting procedures.

### 3.4 Reduced Social Participation and Social Support
The Diabetes-39 instrument used in the MODEL trial (NCT02957513) explicitly includes a **social burden** domain. The REAL study (NCT02214641) measured activity participation, and the MenDIII trial (NCT06574035) assessed social support. These trials consistently show that diabetes reduces social engagement—fewer community activities, reduced social networks, and diminished social support. Voting is fundamentally a social and civic act; reduced social participation directly diminishes the likelihood of voting.

### 3.5 Health Disparities and Intersectional Disadvantage
Several trials focus on populations facing compounded disadvantage: African-American adults in medically underserved areas (NCT02957513), young minority adults (NCT02214641), Black men with depression (NCT06574035), and adults with serious mental illness plus diabetes (NCT04481737). These populations already face systemic barriers to voting; the additional burden of chronic disease compounds these barriers, creating a multiplier effect on voter suppression.

### 3.6 Disability and Functional Impairment
The peer-delivered illness management trial (NCT04481737) measured disability using the London Handicap Scale, capturing domains of social integration and economic self-sufficiency. The internet-delivered CBT trial (NCT03500237) measured disability using the WHO Disability Assessment Schedule, which includes participation in society as a core domain. These instruments demonstrate that diabetes and related chronic conditions produce measurable disability in the domains most critical to civic participation.

## 4. Policy Recommendations

Based on the clinical trial evidence synthesized above, the following policy interventions are recommended to reduce voting barriers for individuals with diabetes and other chronic health conditions:

### 4.1 Expand No-Excuse Absentee and Mail-In Voting
The physical mobility limitations, transportation barriers, and time constraints documented in the cited trials argue strongly for universal access to absentee voting. No-excuse absentee voting eliminates the need to physically travel to polling places, accommodating individuals whose diabetes complications limit mobility or whose medical appointments conflict with Election Day.

### 4.2 Mandate Accessible Early Voting Periods
Extended early voting periods accommodate the unpredictable nature of chronic disease management—flare-ups, medical appointments, and symptom variability. Multiple days of voting opportunity reduce the risk that a single bad health day disenfranchises a voter.

### 4.3 Improve Polling Place Accessibility
Polling places must meet ADA accessibility standards, including accessible parking, ramps, adequate seating for those who cannot stand in long lines, and accessible voting machines for individuals with vision impairments (a common diabetes complication). Curbside voting should be universally available.

### 4.4 Integrate Voter Registration into Healthcare Settings
Given that individuals with diabetes have frequent contact with the healthcare system (as documented in NCT02957513 and NCT01880476), healthcare settings represent a high-opportunity venue for voter registration. The "Vote-ER" model, which places voter registration resources in clinics and hospitals, should be standardized and funded.

### 4.5 Address the Intersection of Health Disparities and Voting Rights
The trials focusing on African-American, Latino, and other minority populations with diabetes (NCT02957513, NCT02214641, NCT06574035) highlight that health disparities and voting barriers are deeply intertwined. Policies must address both simultaneously—for example, by targeting voting accessibility resources to communities with high chronic disease burden and limited healthcare access.

### 4.6 Fund Research on Health Status and Civic Participation
While the clinical trials cited in this brief provide strong indirect evidence, there is a need for dedicated research examining the direct relationship between chronic disease status, disease severity, and voter turnout. Federal funding agencies (NIH, PCORI) should prioritize studies that measure civic participation as an outcome of chronic disease management interventions.

### 4.7 Protect and Expand Vote-by-Mail for Individuals with Chronic Conditions
States should establish permanent vote-by-mail lists specifically for individuals with documented chronic health conditions, ensuring they are not required to repeatedly request absentee ballots. This reduces the administrative burden on individuals already overwhelmed by disease management tasks.

---

## 5. Conclusion

The clinical trial evidence is clear: diabetes and related chronic conditions diminish quality of life, physical functioning, social participation, and psychological well-being. These impairments, documented across diverse populations and study designs, create tangible barriers to one of the most fundamental civic acts—voting. As the prevalence of diabetes continues to rise, the number of citizens whose ability to vote is constrained by chronic disease will only grow. Policymakers have a responsibility to ensure that the electoral system accommodates the reality of chronic illness, so that health status does not determine political voice.
